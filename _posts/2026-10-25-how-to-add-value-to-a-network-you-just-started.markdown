Most students think networking means asking for something. A mentor’s inbox is full of people who ask. The ones who stand out show up with something to give, and the easiest thing to give is a good response to something the person already said.

That’s how I use YouSpot. It does two jobs for me.

It listens. I give it the topics I care about, and it searches public LinkedIn posts on them. It also checks the authors against my own connections and puts the people I already know at the top, especially the ones I’ve drifted from. Then I read what they wrote and reply to it. If a hospital CIO posts about why a Copilot rollout stalled, I don’t need a pitch. I can say what I saw happen at a similar rollout. That reads as a conversation because it is one.

It researches. Before I write to someone, I pull their work history, education and skills, and read up on their company. The note that follows is specific: “You moved from Stryker to Medtronic and your post mentioned supply constraints. How did that change your first quarter?” Nobody mistakes that for a mass message.

For a student, this works at a much smaller scale than mine. You don’t need 4,000 connections. Start with 50: your professors, the mentors in your Career Circle, the people you met at one event. Pick two or three topics you want to work in. Read what those people post about them, and respond once or twice a week with something you actually thought. A comment that adds a fact, a question or a short story from your own coursework counts. “Great post!” doesn’t.

When you do ask for time, the research is what earns it. Twenty minutes with someone who read your post and your career history is an easy yes. Twenty minutes with a stranger who wants a job is a hard one.

One limit. The tool drafts and finds. It can’t care about the person. If your reply could have been written about anyone, delete it and write one that couldn’t.
